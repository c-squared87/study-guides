## 2. What does it mean to solve an equation?

An **equation** is a mathematical statement that says two expressions have the same value. The equals sign means that the left side and the right side are balanced.

For example:

\[
x+4=10
\]

To solve the equation, we want to find the value of \(x\) that makes the statement true. In this case, \(x=6\), because:

\[
6+4=10
\]

A useful way to think about an equation is as a balance scale. Whatever operation you perform on one side, you must perform on the other side to keep the equation balanced.

### The addition and subtraction properties of equality

If two expressions are equal, adding the same number to both sides keeps them equal. The same is true when subtracting the same number from both sides.

For example:

\[
x+4=10
\]

Subtract 4 from both sides:

\[
x+4-4=10-4
\]

Simplify:

\[
\boxed{x=6}
\]

### The multiplication and division properties of equality

You can also multiply or divide both sides by the same nonzero number without changing the equality.

For example:

\[
3x=18
\]

Divide both sides by 3:

\[
\frac{3x}{3}=\frac{18}{3}
\]

Simplify:

\[
\boxed{x=6}
\]

These properties are the foundation of solving equations. The phrase **do the same thing to both sides** is a useful reminder.

## 3. Solving one-step equations

A one-step equation requires one inverse operation to isolate the variable.

An **inverse operation** undoes another operation:

| Operation in the equation | Inverse operation |
|---|---|
| Addition | Subtraction |
| Subtraction | Addition |
| Multiplication | Division |
| Division | Multiplication |

The goal is to undo the operations attached to the variable until the variable is by itself.

### Worked example 1: Addition

Solve:

\[
x+9=16
\]

Step 1: The equation adds 9 to \(x\). Subtract 9 from both sides.

\[
x+9-9=16-9
\]

Step 2: Simplify.

\[
\boxed{x=7}
\]

### Worked example 2: Multiplication

Solve:

\[
5x=35
\]

Step 1: The variable is multiplied by 5. Divide both sides by 5.

\[
\frac{5x}{5}=\frac{35}{5}
\]

Step 2: Simplify.

\[
\boxed{x=7}
\]

### Worked example 3: Division

Solve:

\[
\frac{x}{4}=-3
\]

Step 1: The variable is divided by 4. Multiply both sides by 4.

\[
4\left(\frac{x}{4}\right)=(-3)(4)
\]

Step 2: Simplify.

\[
\boxed{x=-12}
\]

**Check:** \(-12\div4=-3\), so the solution works.

## 4. Solving two-step equations

A two-step equation usually has a variable multiplied by a number and then has a number added or subtracted.

The safest approach is to undo operations in reverse order. If the expression multiplies first and adds second, undo the addition first and then undo the multiplication.

### Worked example 4: A two-step equation

Solve:

\[
3x+5=20
\]

Step 1: Undo the addition of 5 by subtracting 5 from both sides.

\[
3x+5-5=20-5
\]

\[
3x=15
\]

Step 2: Undo multiplication by 3 by dividing both sides by 3.

\[
\frac{3x}{3}=\frac{15}{3}
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{x=5}
\]

### Worked example 5: A negative coefficient

Solve:

\[
-4x+6=18
\]

Step 1: Subtract 6 from both sides.

\[
-4x=12
\]

Step 2: Divide both sides by -4.

\[
\boxed{x=-3}
\]

Notice that dividing a positive number by a negative number gives a negative result.

## 5. Solving multi-step equations

Some equations require several skills from earlier lessons. You may need to distribute, combine like terms, and then use inverse operations.

A dependable order is:

1. Distribute when parentheses require it.
2. Combine like terms on each side.
3. Move variable terms to one side of the equation.
4. Move constants to the other side.
5. Divide or multiply to isolate the variable.
6. Check the result in the original equation.

### Worked example 6: Distribution and combining like terms

Solve:

\[
2(x+3)+4=18
\]

Step 1: Distribute the 2.

\[
2x+6+4=18
\]

Step 2: Combine the constants on the left.

\[
2x+10=18
\]

Step 3: Subtract 10 from both sides.

\[
2x=8
\]

Step 4: Divide both sides by 2.

\[
\boxed{x=4}
\]

### Worked example 7: Variables on both sides

Solve:

\[
5x-4=2x+8
\]

Step 1: Subtract \(2x\) from both sides to collect variable terms on the left.

\[
3x-4=8
\]

Step 2: Add 4 to both sides.

\[
3x=12
\]

Step 3: Divide by 3.

\[
\boxed{x=4}
\]

You can choose to move the variable terms to either side. The important thing is to perform the same operation on both sides and keep track of signs.

### When an equation has no solution or infinitely many solutions

Not every equation produces one numerical answer.

Consider:

\[
4x+7=4x+1
\]

Subtract \(4x\) from both sides:

\[
7=1
\]

That statement is never true. There is **no solution**.

Now consider:

\[
3(x+2)=3x+6
\]

Distribute:

\[
3x+6=3x+6
\]

Both sides are identical. Every value of \(x\) makes the equation true, so there are **infinitely many solutions**. This is also called an identity.

For now, recognize these outcomes. More complicated equations can produce them too.

## 6. Equations containing fractions

When an equation contains fractions, you can often clear the fractions by multiplying every term on both sides by the least common denominator (LCD).

The **least common denominator** is the smallest positive number that is a multiple of all the denominators.

### Worked example 8: Solving an equation with a fraction

Solve:

\[
\frac{x}{3}+2=6
\]

Step 1: Subtract 2 from both sides.

\[
\frac{x}{3}=4
\]

Step 2: Multiply both sides by 3.

\[
x=12
\]

Final answer:

\[
\boxed{x=12}
\]

### Worked example 9: Clearing multiple fractions

Solve:

\[
\frac{x}{2}+\frac{x}{3}=5
\]

Step 1: The denominators are 2 and 3. Their least common denominator is 6.

Step 2: Multiply every term on both sides by 6.

\[
6\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)+6\left(\frac{x}{3}\right)=6(5)
\]

Step 3: Simplify each term.

\[
3x+2x=30
\]

Step 4: Combine like terms and solve.

\[
5x=30
\]

\[
\boxed{x=6}
\]

Multiplying every term by the LCD removes the denominators while preserving the equality.

## 7. Checking a solution

After solving an equation, substitute your answer into the **original equation**. If the left side and right side have the same value, your answer is correct.

### Worked example 10: Checking a solution

Suppose you solved:

\[
2x+3=15
\]

and found \(x=6\).

Substitute 6 for \(x\):

\[
2(6)+3=15
\]

\[
12+3=15
\]

\[
15=15
\]

The statement is true, so \(x=6\) is a solution.

Checking is especially useful when an equation contains several steps. It can catch arithmetic mistakes that are easy to overlook.

## 8. Introduction to inequalities

An **inequality** compares two quantities that may not be equal. Instead of an equals sign, it uses one of these symbols:

| Symbol | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| \(<\) | Less than | \(x<5\) |
| \(>\) | Greater than | \(x>5\) |
| \(\leq\) | Less than or equal to | \(x\leq5\) |
| \(\geq\) | Greater than or equal to | \(x\geq5\) |

An equation often has one solution. An inequality commonly has a range of solutions.

For example, \(x>3\) means all real numbers greater than 3. The values 4, 10, and 3.1 work. The value 3 does not.

### Solving inequalities

You can solve inequalities using many of the same steps as equations:

- Add the same number to both sides.
- Subtract the same number from both sides.
- Multiply or divide both sides by a positive number.

There is one important difference:

**When you multiply or divide both sides of an inequality by a negative number, reverse the inequality symbol.**

For example, \(<\) becomes \(>\), and \(\leq\) becomes \(\geq\).

### Worked example 11: A basic inequality

Solve:

\[
2x+3<11
\]

Step 1: Subtract 3 from both sides.

\[
2x<8
\]

Step 2: Divide both sides by positive 2. The inequality symbol stays the same.

\[
\boxed{x<4}
\]

### Worked example 12: Dividing by a negative number

Solve:

\[
-3x\geq12
\]

Step 1: Divide both sides by -3.

Because we divide by a negative number, reverse the inequality symbol from \(\geq\) to \(\leq\).

\[
\boxed{x\leq-4}
\]

A quick check helps. Try \(x=-5\):

\[
-3(-5)=15\geq12
\]

That is true. Try \(x=0\):

\[
0\geq12
\]

That is false, so the solution values are on the lower side of -4.

## 9. Graphing inequalities on a number line

A number line shows which values satisfy an inequality.

- Use an **open circle** when the endpoint is not included: \(<\) or \(>\).
- Use a **closed circle** when the endpoint is included: \(\leq\) or \(\geq\).
- Shade or draw the line toward the values that satisfy the inequality.

Examples:

| Inequality | Endpoint | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| \(x<4\) | Open circle at 4 | Left |
| \(x\leq4\) | Closed circle at 4 | Left |
| \(x>4\) | Open circle at 4 | Right |
| \(x\geq4\) | Closed circle at 4 | Right |

A useful way to remember the direction is that smaller numbers are to the left and larger numbers are to the right.

## 10. Common mistakes to avoid

| Common mistake | Correct approach |
|---|---|
| Adding 5 to one side only | Perform the same operation on both sides. |
| Solving \(3x=18\) by subtracting 3 | Divide both sides by 3. |
| Forgetting to distribute to every term | Multiply the outside factor by each term in the parentheses. |
| Combining unlike terms | Combine only terms with matching variable parts. |
| Losing a negative sign | Write each operation on a separate line and check the signs. |
| Dividing an inequality by a negative without changing its symbol | Reverse the inequality symbol. |
| Using a closed circle for \(x<4\) | Use an open circle because 4 is not included. |
| Not checking the answer | Substitute the proposed solution into the original equation. |

## 12. Knowledge check

**2. What operation should you use to solve \(4x=28\)?**

**5. When you divide both sides of an inequality by a negative number, what must you do?**

A. Keep the inequality symbol unchanged.

B. Reverse the inequality symbol.

C. Replace the inequality symbol with an equals sign.

D. Change the signs of all the numbers but not the symbol.

**6. Which graph description matches \(x\leq3\)?**

A. Open circle at 3, shaded right.

B. Open circle at 3, shaded left.

C. Closed circle at 3, shaded right.

D. Closed circle at 3, shaded left.

**7. Why is it useful to substitute a proposed solution into the original equation?**

A. It makes the equation shorter.

B. It proves that every equation has a solution.

C. It checks whether the proposed value makes the original statement true.

D. It changes an equation into an inequality.

### Knowledge check answer key

1. **B.** A solution is a value that makes the original equation true.
2. **D.** Division by 4 undoes multiplication by 4.
3. **B.** The 2 must multiply both terms inside the parentheses: \(2x+10\).
4. **C.** If both sides simplify to the same statement, every value of the variable works. This is an identity.
5. **B.** Multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative number reverses the inequality symbol.
6. **D.** The closed circle includes 3, and shading left represents values less than 3.
7. **C.** Substitution checks whether the proposed answer makes the original equation true.
